## What it is

You plot the yields of bonds that only differ by maturity. Then to make decisions you take snapshots of many over time, to see what the market's pricing in. 
The short end, 3m to 2y, is basically the policy end. It's where the central bank has set rates and where the market reckons the next few meetings go. There isn't room for much else in there.
The long end is that plus term premium, it's the extra yield you want for lending out ten years. You can't forecast inflation that far out, you don't know what supply looks like, and you might need to sell in a bad month. Add all that up and you get the upward slope on the right you see often. 

## Vocabulary

Bullish is prices up, yields down. Bearish is prices down, yields up. 

Steepener is the gap between long and short getting wider, flattener is it getting narrower. 

- Bull steepener, front end rallies harder, so the market's pricing cuts.
- Bear steepener, long end sells off harder, so it's term premium or supply or an inflation scare.
- Bear flattener, front end sells off harder, so hikes are getting priced.
- Bull flattener, long end rallies harder, so markets worried about growth.


## Inversion

Short yields above long yields. Happens when policy gets high enough that the market starts pricing cuts down the line, and since the long end is an average of expected short rates, that average ends up below where policy is sitting now. Not all the time, if the lag is long and it jumps around (like in 2022 to 2024 was inverted for two years), sometimes nothing comes with it. Need context. 

## The three theories

First one is pure expectations, where long rates are just the avg of expected short rates and nothing else. Doesn't work on its own, because it needs you to not care whether you roll 3m paper for ten years or buy the 10y outright. This doesn't take into account term premium. 

Then segmentation, or preferred habitat, where investors sit in fixed parts of the curve. Pensions and insurers want long duration to match their liabilities, money funds sit at the front.

Third is liquidity preference, or term premium, makes the most sense, as it's a mixture of the two.
